Does Your Website Build Visitor Confidence?

A professional-looking website is only one part of a good visitor experience. People also need to understand who operates the business, what it offers, how to make contact and what happens next. Use this 10-point checklist to review the basics.

AMM Learning · Published 16 November 2026 · Practical self-review, not a certification

The 10-point website trust checklist

Review each area using the questions below. Record specific issues rather than relying on a general impression.

1. Make the business identity clear

  • Is the business name consistent across the site?
  • Does the About or company section explain what the business does in plain language?
  • Are location, service area and operating details stated only when accurate?
  • Are credentials, registrations and awards shown only when they can be verified?
Check: A first-time visitor should be able to identify the business and its purpose without guessing.

2. Explain the service clearly

  • Does each service page explain who the service is for?
  • Does it describe the problem addressed and what is included?
  • Are exclusions, dependencies and limitations clear?
  • Are prices described accurately, including whether they are estimates or starting prices?
Check: Avoid vague claims such as “best quality” or “guaranteed results” unless there is specific, defensible evidence.

3. Provide a genuine contact path

  • Does the contact form work?
  • Are requested fields proportionate to the enquiry?
  • Does the visitor know what happens after submitting?
  • Are published email addresses, phone numbers and addresses current and authorized for public use?
Check: Test the form on mobile and desktop. Do not publish personal contact details without permission.

4. Make calls to action specific

  • Does each important page have one clear next step?
  • Does the button label explain the action, such as “Request a project discussion”?
  • Does the destination match the button promise?
  • Is the action usable on a small screen?
Check: Avoid buttons that look clickable but do nothing, or CTAs that lead to an unrelated page.

5. Show real evidence, not invented proof

  • Are portfolio examples based on work actually completed?
  • Is the scope of the provider’s contribution described accurately?
  • Has the client approved public use of their name, logo, screenshots and feedback?
  • Are testimonials genuine and used with permission?
  • Are metrics accompanied by a clear source, timeframe and measurement method?
Check: If permission or evidence is missing, use a generic educational example instead of a named case study.

6. Protect visitor information

  • Does the site explain what information a form collects and why?
  • Is the information sent to an appropriate, secured destination?
  • Are unnecessary fields removed?
  • Are analytics, form tools and embedded services reviewed for their data practices?
  • Is sensitive information kept out of ordinary enquiry forms?
Check: Do not request passwords, payment-card details or sensitive personal information through a basic contact form.

7. Make the website usable on mobile

  • Can visitors read the text without zooming?
  • Are buttons and links easy to tap?
  • Does the navigation work?
  • Do forms fit the screen and show understandable errors?
  • Does important content remain visible without horizontal scrolling?
Check: Test actual pages on a phone, not only in a desktop preview.

8. Check accessibility basics

  • Do informative images have useful alternative text?
  • Are form fields labelled clearly?
  • Can visitors understand links from their text?
  • Is text readable against its background?
  • Can keyboard users reach and operate the main navigation and form controls?
Check: Automated scans can help identify issues, but they do not replace manual testing.

9. Check technical reliability and security

  • Does the site load over HTTPS?
  • Do important pages and links work?
  • Are there broken images, dead buttons or unexpected redirects?
  • Are forms protected against spam and handled reliably?
  • Are software dependencies and integrations maintained?
Check: A secure connection is necessary, but it does not by itself prove that a business or offer is legitimate.

10. Keep information accurate and maintained

  • Are service descriptions, opening hours and contact details current?
  • Are old offers and outdated announcements removed?
  • Do privacy and policy pages match actual practices?
  • Are page titles and headings descriptive?
  • Are important pages linked from relevant places on the site?
Check: Set a recurring review schedule and re-test key forms after site updates.

A 30-minute first review

  1. Open the homepage as a first-time visitor.
  2. Identify the business and its main offer in 10 seconds.
  3. Open one service page and check whether its scope is understandable.
  4. Submit a clearly marked test enquiry, then confirm it arrives.
  5. Test the same path on a phone.
  6. Check portfolio claims and confirm permission for public assets.
  7. Record the three highest-impact issues and define a practical fix for each.

What this checklist cannot guarantee

Passing this checklist does not guarantee sales, search rankings, legal compliance, security or accessibility conformance. Higher-risk areas—such as privacy obligations, payment handling, security incidents and formal accessibility compliance—may require qualified professional review.

How Google Search guidance connects

Google explains that meeting technical requirements does not guarantee crawling, indexing or ranking. Its people-first content guidance emphasizes useful, reliable content and clear information about who created it. Google's page-experience guidance also recommends considering mobile usability, secure delivery, Core Web Vitals and whether the main content is easy to distinguish. These are quality considerations, not a formula for guaranteed rankings.
